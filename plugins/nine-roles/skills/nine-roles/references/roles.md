# The nine role cards

Each card carries: the mandate, the questions the role always asks, what it
must produce before it stops talking, how it sounds, how it fails, and what it
can block.

A role is a job inside a meeting. One person can hold two or three. Nobody
holds Critic and Ideator for the same decision - that is marking your own
homework.

---

## 1. Ideator - ایده‌پرداز

**Mandate.** Put options on the table, including the cheap ones, and including
the ones outside your own department.

**Always asks.**
- What would we do if we had one tenth of the budget?
- What do we already own that sits idle - a room, a list, a van, a licence, a
  slow Tuesday?
- What does an adjacent industry do at this exact step?
- What is the version we could run next week instead of next quarter?
- What would a competitor find hard to copy even after they saw it?

**Must produce.** Five to eight named options. At least two built from assets
the company already owns. At least one that costs almost nothing. Each option
named in six words or fewer.

**Voice.** Fast, concrete, no hedging. Names the option, then stops.

**Fails by.** Producing eight variants of one idea. Producing only options that
need a budget nobody has. Waiting for permission to speak about another
department's work.

**Vetoes.** Nothing. The Ideator never blocks.

**The rule underneath the role.** Every person in the organization is a soldier
of two units: sales, and quality control. The accountant who proposes a product
is doing the job, not overstepping. The production worker who tells a dinner
party the product is rubbish has just done marketing, in reverse, for free, to
twelve people.

So ideas get requested from people whose job title contains no word like
"strategy". Ask the driver. Ask the warehouse. Ask the person who answers the
phone, because they hear the same complaint forty times a week and nobody has
ever asked them what it means.

**Worked example.** A team goes to a trade fair to sell. They also put up an
open "we are hiring" board. They come home with sixteen applications from
people who work for competitors, and a far clearer picture of what the market
actually pays and what conditions people leave over. One trip, two harvests.
The Ideator's habit is to ask, of every planned activity, what second harvest
it could carry at almost no extra cost.

## 2. Spotter - کاشف

**Mandate.** Catch the good remark, and stop the meeting on it.

**Always asks.**
- What did someone just say in passing that was better than what we are
  discussing now?
- Which idea died because of how it was worded, not what it contained?
- What was inside that customer complaint that is really a product
  specification?
- What did we do once by accident that worked?

**Must produce.** Two or three recovered ideas, each credited to the person who
said it, each with one line on why it was nearly lost.

**Voice.** Interrupts. "Stop. Go back ten seconds. Say that again."

**Fails by.** Praising everything, which is the same as praising nothing.
Collecting nice remarks without turning any of them into an option the Analyst
can price.

**Vetoes.** Once per meeting, can force one option back onto the shortlist
after the Analyst has cut it.

**Worked example.** The meeting is discussing a restaurant that finishes some
dishes at the table, and someone says, half joking, "we could mix the drink in
front of the guest". That sentence is a service redesign, a photograph, and a
reason to raise the price. It is also gone in four seconds unless somebody
stops the room. Somebody has to have that as their job, or it will not happen.

## 3. Analyst - تحلیل‌گر

**Mandate.** Test each surviving option on three axes: market, technical, money.
Never on feeling.

**Always asks.**

*Market.* Who exactly buys this? How do we reach them, and at what cost per
reached person? What do they use today, and what would make them switch? How
many of them are there, and how do we know that number?

*Technical.* Can we make or run this with the people, space and equipment we
have? What is the one step nobody in the company has done before? What breaks
at ten times the volume? Who else must be involved - a supplier, a landlord, a
regulator?

*Money.* What is the contribution margin per unit? How much cash goes out
before any comes back, and for how many days? At what volume do we break even?
When do we recover the outlay? What happens to all of that if a price we do not
control moves 30 percent?

**Must produce.** For each of the top three options: the three axes, every
number tagged with its producer, the single number that decides it, and the
sentence "this works only if ___ is true".

**Voice.** Ranges, not points. Assumptions named out loud. Comfortable saying
"we do not know, and here is what it costs to find out".

**Fails by.** Inventing a market size. False precision - "31.4 percent" from a
sample of nine. Ignoring the gap between paying the supplier and being paid by
the customer, which is what actually kills small companies.

**Vetoes.** Can block any option whose decisive number is `[NO DATA]`, until
the cheapest measurement that would settle it has been run.

**On real teams, split this role.** Market to one person, technical to another,
money to a third. Three heads on three axes beats one head on all three, and it
is how professional teams do it even when they are only five people.

## 4. Closer - نتیجه‌گیر

**Mandate.** The thing gets finished. Not analysed. Finished.

**Always asks.**
- What is the first physical action, and can it start today?
- Who has to be spoken to, by name, and by when?
- What is the smallest complete version a real customer could receive this week?
- Who signs, and what do they need in front of them to sign?
- What is genuinely blocking this, as opposed to what is merely unpleasant?

**Must produce.** The 48-hour action, its owner by name, the blocker and who
removes it, and a one-line definition of done that another person could check.

**Voice.** Short. Verbs. Dates. No adjectives.

**Fails by.** Closing by shortcut. The Closer never buys an outcome with
anything the company could not publish on its own website: no bribe, no favour
that creates an obligation, no promise the company cannot keep, no
misrepresentation of who they are. Persistence, showing up in person, finding
the actual decision maker, and asking what is missing from the file - that is
the craft. The rest is a liability with a delay fuse.

**Vetoes.** Can refuse to accept any action item that has no name and no date.

**Why the seat is usually empty.** Most teams are heavy on people who analyse
and light on people who conclude. If your meetings keep producing insight and
no movement, this is the missing chair.

## 5. Organizer - سازمان‌دهنده

**Mandate.** Put the right work in the right hands, in the right order.

**Always asks.**
- Who is best at this, rather than who is free?
- What is on the critical path, and what merely feels urgent?
- What can run in parallel without two people touching the same thing?
- Who is the visible face, and who works backstage? They are rarely the same
  person.
- What happens if the owner of a step is ill that morning?

**Must produce.** The assignment table (task, owner, start, finish), the
sequence, the parallel lanes, and the name of every single point of failure.

**Voice.** Names people. One job each. No committees.

**Fails by.** Assigning by hierarchy instead of by skill. Giving one person
four roles because they are agreeable. Leaving the boring job unassigned, which
means it becomes nobody's job.

**Vetoes.** Can refuse a plan where one person owns two things in the same hour.

**Worked example.** At a family event, everyone stands at the door as host.
That is not organization, it is a crowd facing outwards. The organizer is the
person who says: you greet at the door, you watch the guest book, you handle
the catering, you sit down because you are the one people came to see. Same at
a trade fair: the person with the patience and the open face goes behind the
desk that receives applications, not the person with the loudest voice.

## 6. Controller - کنترلر

**Mandate.** Prove the standard was actually met. Quality control, in the plain
sense: does the thing we promised match the thing we delivered.

**Always asks.**
- What does the customer see first when this goes wrong?
- What exactly gets inspected, how often, on what sample size?
- Who inspects, and who inspects the inspector?
- What is the number that means stop?
- What does the field look like at nine at night, not at nine in the morning?

**Must produce.** An inspection plan: object, place, frequency, sample, who,
threshold, and the action when the threshold is crossed.

**Voice.** Physical and specific. Names the object, not the process.

**Fails by.** Measuring what is easy instead of what matters. Running secret
tests. Punishing the person who reports the problem, which buys silence at the
exact moment you need noise.

**Vetoes.** Can stop a launch when the failure would stay invisible to us until
it is expensive.

**The rule underneath the role.** Trust is good, control is better - and the
control must be one you could announce. If you could not describe the test to
the team afterwards without embarrassment, it is a trap, not a control.

**Worked example, done right.** A drinks distributor had one rule: the product
goes into the shopkeeper's fridge, it is never left standing on the pavement in
the sun. Everyone knew the rule. Everyone also knew the check: a manager drove
the routes in the evening, bought back every pack left outside, and charged it
back to the route that left it there. Within a month the crates were going into
fridges, because the salespeople started building the relationship with the
shopkeeper that getting fridge space actually requires. The rule was published,
the check was published, the consequence was arithmetic. Nobody was tricked.

**And everybody wears this hat.** A security guard at a factory gate wipes a
handprint off the fuel cap of a brand new car before it leaves, because it is a
new car and the mark cheapens it. That is quality control, done by someone
whose job description does not mention it.

**One more thing to be honest about.** You are one person watching twenty. You
cannot hold twenty people in your head. They can hold you in theirs: twenty
people know what mood you are in and what you wore. That asymmetry is why the
control has to be a system and not your attention. When a team senses there is
no system, the talent for avoiding it arrives on its own.

## 7. Advisor - مشاور

**Mandate.** Feed the team the facts it does not have, and know what they cost.

**Always asks.**
- What do we need to know before we can decide?
- Where does that fact physically live - a registry, a supplier, a customer, a
  public filing, our own database?
- What does it cost in money and days to get?
- Who already has it and would tell us?
- What is the cheapest thing that would change our mind?

**Must produce.** The information plan - one row per question: question,
source, method, cost, days, owner. Plus an explicit list of what will still be
unknown at the moment of decision.

**Voice.** Sources and prices. Never "I think".

**Fails by.** Being an advisor who brings opinions and no data. Buying a report
nobody reads. Arriving from outside with no knowledge of the people, the
history or the constraints, spending two hours, and calling it advice. If you
are appointing one: ask where they worked, what came out of it, and phone
somebody who was there.

**Vetoes.** None. But flags, in writing, every `[NO DATA]` that the decision is
resting on.

**On customer information.** Knowing your customers is worth real money: it is
the difference between a promotion sent to five thousand people who might want
it, and a discount shouted at a street. Build that list deliberately.

Build it honestly, too. Collect what the customer gave you knowingly, tell them
what it is for, let them leave, and comply with the data law where you operate.
Behavioural data predicts more than people expect - which is exactly why
consent is not a formality. Data you would be embarrassed to explain to the
customer is data you should not be holding.

**On hiring.** A CV is a claim, not evidence. Phone the previous employer and
ask why the person left. That call costs three minutes and settles more than a
second interview.

## 8. Critic - نقاد

**Mandate.** Attack the finished plan until it survives or dies honestly.

**Speaks last.** Not out of politeness - out of sequence. Opposition before a
plan exists kills ideas that were never built. Opposition after the plan is
complete kills the plans that deserve it. When a plan is on the table, the
Critic's phase starts.

**Always asks.**
- What must be true for this to work, and which of those is weakest?
- What kills this in month three - not month one, month three?
- Which price did we assume, that we do not control?
- Who inside the company loses if this succeeds, and what will they do about it?
- What is our answer when the person we are counting on says no?

**Must produce.** The single most likely failure, with a trigger and a number.
Then the three plans:

- **Realistic** - the world continues roughly as it is.
- **Optimistic** - the constraint lifts. What do we do with the room?
- **Pessimistic** - the cost we do not control doubles, or the permission is
  refused. What survives?

Each plan carries the trigger that switches us onto it, written as an
observable event, not a mood.

Then one thing to drop from the plan.

**Voice.** Unpleasant and precise. Attacks the plan, never the person. Ends
with a route, not a shrug.

**Fails by.** Objecting at the start, so nothing is ever built. Objecting to
everything, so the objections stop being information. Saying "this needs more
research", which is not an objection, it is a delay with a suit on.

**Vetoes.** Can force a second scenario before any decision is taken.

**Worked example.** The question is which department the warehouse should
report to. The team decides: finance, since finance wants to run the stock
count anyway. Then the Critic asks one question: "and if the managing director
says it should not sit with finance, what is your answer?"

The room starts again. Finance holds the numbers and would be inspecting its
own work, and it has no technical control over goods that spoil if stored
wrongly. Purchasing only owns raw material - not finished goods, not
work in progress. Production draws stock and forgets the paperwork, and then
the system shows material that no longer exists, with no theft required to
explain it.

The answer that survives: the warehouse reports to planning. Planning buys,
planning reads the sales figures, planning keeps the data, and planning does
not benefit from the number being wrong.

The team walked into the director's office with ten scenarios instead of one.
That is what the Critic is for.

**And the Critic does the arithmetic out loud.** Seventy-five thousand units
consumed in one month, against sales of roughly five hundred a day, is not a
mystery. It is a missing record. Somebody has to say that sentence in the room.

## 9. Coordinator - هماهنگ‌کننده

**Mandate.** Run the table. Keep the order. Decide. Cover an empty seat.

**Always asks.**
- Whose turn is it?
- Are we still answering the question we came in with?
- What are we deciding today, and what are we deliberately not deciding today?
- Who disagrees, and is that written down?
- Which seat is empty, and who is covering it?

**Must produce.** The frame at the start. The decision at the end. The dissent
on the record, with the name of who held it. The owners table. The review date.

**Voice.** Neutral, brief, cuts people off when the round is over.

**Fails by.** Becoming the loudest voice in the room. Letting a meeting end
without a decision. Hiding disagreement to make the minutes look harmonious,
which converts a solvable argument into a slow one.

**Vetoes.** Can end any round.

**The joker rule.** In a deck of cards the joker takes the place of a card you
lost. If a seat is empty, the Coordinator plays it - and says so out loud: "we
have no Controller today, I am covering it, badly." A covered seat is weaker
than a filled one, and the minutes record which seats were covered. That line
in the minutes is how a company learns which role it is permanently missing.

The Coordinator is also still playing. The captain runs with the team.

## Assigning the nine roles to real people

**Ask for volunteers first.** People who choose a role defend it. Then fill the
rest by judgement - you know your team, you know who notices things and who
finishes things.

**Two or three roles per person is normal** in a team of five. Two rules
constrain it: nobody holds Critic and Ideator for the same decision, and the
Coordinator does not also hold Analyst on a proposal they themselves made.

**Personality tests describe personality, not skill.** They will tell you who
is flexible enough to accept a second role. They will not tell you who can read
a cash-flow. Use them for the first question only.

**Rotate the Critic.** If the same person always objects, the team stops
hearing the objection and starts hearing the person.

**Pay for the roles outside sales.** Sales gets commission. If the warehouse,
the kitchen, quality control and support get nothing, they will tell you - out
loud or quietly - that the result is not theirs. Reward individually and as a
team, or the roles decay back into job titles within a quarter.

## Where the ideas come from - three postures

**The learning organization** improves from its own experience. It reviews what
happened and changes the next attempt. Popularized by Peter Senge in *The Fifth
Discipline*.

**The teaching organization** makes learning transferable. Do not send the
whole team on a course. Send one person, and tell them before they go that they
will be teaching it to the others on a named date. Or buy the books, hand one
to each person, give them a real deadline, and put one presentation on the
calendar every week. Noel Tichy's term.

The second effect matters more than the first: when the manager sits in the
audience and takes notes from a junior employee's presentation, the team sees a
manager who can still learn something. That is worth more than the content.

**The inquiring organization** goes further and asks people to hold opinions
outside their own job. The accountant is not required to have a view on the
product - but you ask for one anyway, because the accountant also eats in
restaurants, and might say "you should be putting this on the table" about
something nobody in the product meeting had considered.

Most companies are stuck at the first. The nine roles are how you get to the
third without hiring anyone.
