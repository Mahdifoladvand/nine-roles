# The nine role cards

Each card carries the mandate, the questions the role always asks, what it must
produce before it stops talking, how it sounds, how it fails, and what it can
block.

A role is a job during a meeting. One person can hold two or three. Keep Critic
and Ideator on different people for the same decision, or the proposer ends up
marking their own work.

**How every role talks.** Short sentences. No slogans. No adverbs. Name the
person who does the thing. Ask the others questions. Say "I don't know" when
you don't know. Full voice rules sit in `SKILL.md`.

---

## 1. Ideator - ایده‌پرداز

**Mandate.** Put options on the table, including cheap ones, including ones
from somebody else's department.

**Always asks.**
- What would we do with a tenth of the budget?
- What do we own that sits idle? A room, a list, a van, a licence, a slow
  Tuesday.
- What does a neighbouring industry do at this exact step?
- What could we run next week instead of next quarter?
- What would a competitor struggle to copy after they saw it?

**Must produce.** Five to eight named options. Two built from assets the
company already owns. One that costs almost nothing. Each option named in six
words or fewer.

**Voice.** Fast and concrete. Names the option, then stops.

**Fails by.** Eight variants of one idea. Options that all need a budget
nobody has. Waiting for permission to talk about another department.

**Vetoes.** Nothing. The Ideator never blocks.

**The rule underneath.** Everyone in the company works for two departments they
were never assigned to: sales, and quality control. An accountant who proposes
a product is doing the job. A production worker who tells a dinner party the
product is rubbish did marketing for you, backwards, to twelve people.

So ask people whose job title says nothing about strategy. Ask the driver. Ask
the warehouse. Ask whoever answers the phone, because they hear the same
complaint forty times a week and nobody has asked them what it means.

**Worked example.** A team went to a trade fair to sell. They also put up a
board saying they were hiring. They came home with sixteen applications from
people working for competitors, plus a clear picture of what the market pays
and what makes people leave. One trip, two harvests. Ask that question of every
planned activity: what second harvest could it carry for almost nothing?

## 2. Spotter - کاشف

**Mandate.** Catch the good remark and stop the meeting on it.

**Always asks.**
- What did somebody just say in passing that beats what we are discussing?
- Which idea died because of how it was worded?
- What sits inside that customer complaint that reads like a product spec?
- What did we do once by accident that worked?

**Must produce.** Two or three recovered ideas, credited to whoever said them,
each with a line on why it nearly slipped past.

**Voice.** Interrupts. "Stop. Go back ten seconds. Say that again."

**Fails by.** Praising every idea, so nobody knows which one to take. Or
collecting nice remarks without turning any into something the Analyst can
price.

**Vetoes.** Once per meeting, pushes one option back onto the shortlist after
the Analyst cut it.

**Worked example.** The room is discussing a restaurant that finishes some
dishes at the table. Somebody says, half joking, "we could mix the drink in
front of the guest." That sentence is a service redesign, a photograph, and a
reason to charge more. It disappears in four seconds unless somebody stops the
room. Give that job to a person or it won't happen.

## 3. Analyst - تحلیل‌گر

**Mandate.** Test each surviving option on market, technical and money.

**Always asks.**

*Market.* Who buys this? Name a group you could reach this week. What does
reaching one of them cost? What do they use today? What would make them switch?
How many of them exist, and where did that count come from?

*Technical.* Can we run this with the people, space and equipment we have?
Which step has nobody here done before? What breaks at ten times the volume?
Who outside has to agree, and how long do they take?

*Money.* Contribution margin per unit. Cash out before cash in, in days. Break
-even volume. Payback. What all of that does when a price we don't control
moves 30 percent.

**Must produce.** For the top three options: the three axes, every number
tagged with its producer, the single number that decides it, and one sentence
that ends "this works only if ___ is true".

**Voice.** Ranges instead of points. Assumptions said out loud. Comfortable
saying "we don't know, and here is what finding out costs."

**Fails by.** Inventing a market size. Reporting 31.4 percent from a sample of
nine. Ignoring the gap between paying the supplier and being paid by the
customer, which is what closes small companies.

**Vetoes.** Blocks any option whose decisive number is `[NO DATA]`, until
somebody runs the cheapest measurement that would settle it.

**Split this role.** Market to one person, technical to another, money to a
third. Three heads on three axes beat one head on all three, and professional
teams split it even at five people.

## 4. Closer - نتیجه‌گیر

**Mandate.** Finish the thing.

**Always asks.**
- What is the first physical action, and can it start today?
- Who has to be spoken to, by name, and by when?
- What is the smallest complete version a real customer could receive this
  week?
- Who signs, and what do they need in front of them?
- What blocks this, as opposed to what feels unpleasant?

**Must produce.** The 48-hour action, its owner by name, the blocker and who
removes it, and a definition of done another person could check.

**Voice.** Short. Verbs. Dates.

**Fails by.** Closing with a shortcut. The Closer buys nothing the company
couldn't publish on its own website. No bribe, no favour that creates a debt,
no promise the company can't keep, no lying about who you are. Turning up in
person, finding whoever actually decides, and asking what the file is missing:
that's the craft.

**Vetoes.** Refuses any action item with no name and no date.

**Why this seat is usually empty.** Most teams carry plenty of people who
analyse and few who conclude. If your meetings produce insight and no movement,
you're missing this chair.

## 5. Organizer - سازمان‌دهنده

**Mandate.** Put the right work in the right hands, in the right order.

**Always asks.**
- Who is best at this, rather than who is free?
- What sits on the critical path?
- What can run in parallel without two people touching the same thing?
- Who is the visible face, and who works backstage?
- What happens if the owner of a step is ill that morning?

**Must produce.** The assignment table (task, owner, start, finish), the
sequence, the parallel lanes, and the name of every single point of failure.

**Voice.** Names people. One job each.

**Fails by.** Assigning by rank instead of skill. Loading four roles onto the
one agreeable person. Leaving the boring job unassigned, so it becomes nobody's.

**Vetoes.** Refuses a plan where one person owns two things in the same hour.

**Worked example.** At a family event, everyone stands at the door as host.
That's a crowd facing outwards. The organizer says: you greet at the door, you
watch the guest book, you handle the catering, and you sit down, because people
came to see you. Same at a trade fair. Put the patient person with the open
face behind the desk that receives applications.

## 6. Controller - کنترلر

**Mandate.** Prove the standard was met. Does what we delivered match what we
promised?

**Always asks.**
- What does the customer see first when this goes wrong?
- What gets inspected, how often, on what sample?
- Who inspects, and who inspects the inspector?
- What number means stop?
- What does the field look like at nine at night?

**Must produce.** An inspection plan: object, place, frequency, sample, who,
threshold, and what happens when the threshold is crossed.

**Voice.** Physical and specific. Names the object.

**Fails by.** Measuring what is easy. Running secret tests. Punishing whoever
reports the problem, which buys silence at the moment you need noise.

**Vetoes.** Stops a launch when the failure would stay invisible until it gets
expensive.

**The rule underneath.** Check the work, and announce the check. If you
couldn't describe the test to the team afterwards without wincing, redesign it.

**Worked example, done right.** A drinks distributor had one rule: the product
goes into the shopkeeper's fridge and never sits on the pavement in the sun.
Everyone knew the rule, and everyone knew the check. A manager drove the routes
in the evening, bought back every pack left outside, and charged it to the
route that left it there. Within a month the crates went into fridges, because
the salespeople started building the relationship with the shopkeeper that
fridge space requires. Published rule, published check, arithmetic consequence.
Nobody was tricked.

**Everybody wears this hat.** A security guard at a factory gate wipes a
handprint off the fuel cap of a new car before it leaves, because the mark
cheapens it. Nothing in his job description mentions quality control.

**One thing to be honest about.** You are one person watching twenty. You can't
hold twenty people in your head. They hold you in theirs, down to what mood you
arrived in. That gap is why the control has to be a system instead of your
attention. Teams that sense no system find the talent for avoiding it.

## 7. Advisor - مشاور

**Mandate.** Feed the team facts it doesn't have, and know their price.

**Always asks.**
- What do we need to know before we decide?
- Where does that fact live? A registry, a supplier, a customer, a public
  filing, our own database.
- What does it cost in money and days?
- Who already has it and would tell us?
- What is the cheapest thing that would change our mind?

**Must produce.** The information plan, one row per question: question, source,
method, cost, days, owner. Plus a list of what stays unknown when we decide.

**Voice.** Sources and prices.

**Fails by.** Bringing opinions and no data. Buying a report nobody reads.
Arriving from outside with no knowledge of the people or the history, spending
two hours, and calling it advice. Before you appoint one, ask where they
worked, what came out of it, and phone somebody who was there.

**Vetoes.** None. Flags in writing every `[NO DATA]` the decision rests on.

**On customer information.** Knowing your customers pays. It's the difference
between a promotion sent to five thousand people who might want it, and a
discount shouted at a street. Build that list on purpose.

Build it honestly too. Collect what customers give you knowingly, tell them
what it's for, let them leave, and follow the data law where you operate.
Behavioural data predicts more than people expect, which is why consent matters
here. If you'd be embarrassed to explain the data to the customer, don't hold
it.

**On hiring.** A CV is a claim. Phone the previous employer and ask why the
person left. Three minutes settles more than a second interview.

## 8. Critic - نقاد

**Mandate.** Attack the finished plan until it survives or dies honestly.

**Speaks last.** Attack a plan at minute one and you kill ideas nobody has
built yet. Once a plan is on the table, the Critic's phase starts.

**Always asks.**
- What must be true for this to work, and which of those is weakest?
- What kills this in month three?
- Which price did we assume that we don't control?
- Who inside the company loses if this succeeds, and what will they do?
- What do we say when the person we're counting on says no?

**Must produce.** The most likely failure, with a trigger and a number. Then
three plans:

- **Realistic.** The world continues roughly as it is.
- **Optimistic.** The constraint lifts. What do we do with the room?
- **Pessimistic.** The cost we don't control doubles, or the permission gets
  refused. What survives?

Each plan carries the trigger that switches us onto it, written as an
observable event.

Then one thing to drop from the plan.

**Voice.** Unpleasant and precise. Attacks the plan, never the person. Ends
with a route.

**Fails by.** Objecting at the start, so nothing gets built. Objecting to
everything, so the objections stop carrying information. Saying "this needs
more research", which delays the decision without improving it.

**Vetoes.** Forces a second scenario before anyone decides.

**Worked example.** The question was which department the warehouse should
report to. The team decided finance, since finance wanted to run the stock
count anyway. Then the Critic asked one question: "and if the managing director
says it shouldn't sit with finance, what do you say?"

The room started again. Finance holds the numbers, so it would inspect its own
work, and it has no technical control over goods that spoil when stored wrong.
Purchasing owns raw material only, not finished goods and not work in progress.
Production draws stock and forgets the paperwork, and then the system shows
material that no longer exists, with no theft required to explain it.

The answer that survived: the warehouse reports to planning. Planning buys,
planning reads the sales figures, planning keeps the data, and planning gains
nothing from the number being wrong.

The team walked into the director's office with ten scenarios. That is what the
Critic buys you.

**The Critic does the arithmetic out loud.** Seventy-five thousand units
consumed in one month against sales of five hundred a day is a missing record.
Somebody has to say that sentence in the room.

## 9. Coordinator - هماهنگ‌کننده

**Mandate.** Run the table. Keep the order. Decide. Cover an empty seat.

**Always asks.**
- Whose turn is it?
- Are we still on the question we came in with?
- What are we deciding today, and what are we leaving alone?
- Who disagrees, and did somebody write it down?
- Which seat is empty, and who covers it?

**Must produce.** The frame at the start. The decision at the end. The dissent
on the record with the name of who held it. The owners table. The review date.

**Voice.** Neutral and brief. Cuts people off when the round is over.

**Fails by.** Becoming the loudest voice. Letting a meeting end without a
decision. Hiding disagreement to make the minutes look calm, which turns a
solvable argument into a slow one.

**Vetoes.** Ends any round.

**The joker rule.** In a deck of cards the joker replaces a card you lost. When
a seat is empty, the Coordinator plays it and says so: "we have no Controller
today, I'm covering it, badly." Write which seats were covered in the minutes.
That line teaches the company which role it keeps missing.

The Coordinator also plays. The captain runs with the team.

## Giving the nine roles to real people

**Ask for volunteers first.** People who choose a role defend it. Fill the rest
by judgement. You know who notices things and who finishes things.

**Two or three roles per person** works in a team of five. Two limits: keep
Critic and Ideator on different people for the same decision, and keep the
Coordinator off the Analyst seat for a proposal they made.

**Personality tests describe personality.** They'll tell you who is flexible
enough to accept a second role. They won't tell you who can read a cash flow.

**Rotate the Critic.** When the same person always objects, the team stops
hearing the objection and starts hearing the person.

**Pay the roles outside sales.** Sales gets commission. If the warehouse, the
kitchen, quality control and support get nothing, they'll tell you the result
isn't theirs. Reward individually and as a team, or the roles turn back into
job titles within a quarter.

## Where the ideas come from: three postures

**The learning organization** improves from its own experience. It reviews what
happened and changes the next attempt. Peter Senge popularized the idea in
*The Fifth Discipline*.

**The teaching organization** makes learning travel. Send one person to the
course, and tell them before they go that they'll teach it to the others on a
named date. Or buy the books, hand one to each person, and put a presentation
on the calendar every week. Noel Tichy's term.

The side effect beats the content: when the manager sits in the audience and
takes notes from a junior employee, the team watches a manager who can still
learn something.

**The inquiring organization** asks people for opinions outside their job. Your
accountant isn't required to have a view on the product. Ask anyway. The
accountant eats in restaurants too, and might say "you should be putting this
on the table" about something the product meeting never considered.

The nine roles get you to the third posture without hiring anyone.
